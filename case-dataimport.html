(01)Brief

Admin tools are where good intentions go to become error messages.

The data management team at Toggl needed a redesigned import experience — the flow admins use to bring their organisation's data into the platform. It sounds quiet. It was not.

This was also my first project at Toggl OÜ (the parent product, distinct from Toggl Hire), joining a 9-designer team after three years as a solo act. A different kind of challenge.

(02)Problem

Data import looks fine in demos. In production, it's a different story.

Files with unexpected columns. Character encoding that works until it doesn't. 40,000 rows and one error on row 39,847 — with a message that says "something went wrong" and no indication of what, where, or whether starting over would help.

A real scenario from user research:
Admin uploads a CSV. Import appears to succeed. Data looks wrong. Admin re-uploads. Data is now duplicated. Admin contacts support. Support resolves it three days later. Admin never fully trusts the platform again.

↑ Existing import flow mapped against failure states and support ticket themes

"Optimised for the happy path. Left everything else as the user's problem."
(03)Approach

I started with the failure states, not the happy path. Error states are where you learn what the product actually knows — and where you discover what engineering can and can't surface to the UI.

My main question to the engineering team, asked many times in many ways: "If something goes wrong here, what can we actually tell the user?" The answer was usually more than we were currently telling them. That gap — between what the system knew and what the interface communicated — was the design problem.

↑ Error state taxonomy — categorising failure modes by what the system could detect

I redesigned the import flow in stages: pre-upload validation (catch what you can before anything's processed), in-progress feedback (honest about what's happening), and post-import review (clear summary of what succeeded, what didn't, and what to do next).

↑ Redesigned import flow — pre-upload validation and progress states

Working within a 9-person design team after three years solo meant learning to advocate loudly for admin experience in a roadmap full of shinier priorities. I got good at framing unglamorous flows in terms of support costs and trust damage.

(04)Outcome

Clearer flows, honest error messages, and one less reason to email support.

The redesigned experience surfaced errors earlier, communicated them more specifically, and gave admins a clear path forward when something went wrong — rather than a generic message and a support ticket number.

↑ Final import flow — validation, progress, and review screens

The broader lesson: admin tools shape how much people trust a product. A confusing import flow doesn't just create support tickets — it creates doubt that persists long after the specific incident is resolved.

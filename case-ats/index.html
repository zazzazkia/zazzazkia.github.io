(01)The challenge

Add everything. Keep it simple.

Toggl Hire started as a single skills test, built for Toggl's own hiring. Over four years I helped grow it into the whole funnel — pipeline, interviews, analytics, careers pages. Each new feature was a chance to make it heavier. The job was to add everything an ATS does, without the weight that usually comes with it.

(02)Pipeline

The home base

The problem

Hiring managers don't just look at a funnel, they run it. Add a test, change the criteria, clear out a stage. In most ATS tools that means leaving the board and digging through settings menus that sit nowhere near the actual work. Every change breaks your flow.

The insight

Managers need one high-trust home base. If they can't see what's running in the background, or act on people at volume without drilling into every profile, they stop trusting that the board reflects what's actually happening.

↑ Assessment settings — manager can tweak the test without leaving the pipeline. The board stays the home base. Configuration comes to it.

↑ Candidate pipeline — each column is a hiring stage. Managers see status at a glance, act inline, move people forward without ceremony.

Design decisions

I put the structural controls on the board itself. Changing test settings happens inline, where you're already working, instead of three menus deep.

I made bulk actions work wherever you're looking, the full board or a single stage. When you're moving through volume, you reject or advance a whole group at once, without needing to open them one by one.

The cards carry their own context too. I made each card highlight the score that matters at that stage. Previous scores stay visible at a glance, so you never click in just to see how someone did before.

Business & UX impact
  • Managers stopped leaving the pipeline to change the settings. They adjust the strategy in place, without losing sight of where everyone stands.
  • In post-launch interviews, managers kept landing on the same relief: the full assessment history sitting right on the card. One said it took the "guesswork and endless clicking" out of their day.
(03)Candidate view

The whole picture

The problem

Hiring is collaborative, which means the evidence ends up everywhere. Teams scored skills in Toggl Hire, read resumes in email, left feedback in Slack. Three places, one decision, no single view of the person. So people decided on whatever fragment happened to be in front of them.

The insight

The problem wasn't too little data, it was scattered data. To decide well, a team needs the whole candidate in one place and organized, so no one's hiring on a hunch or half the story.

↑ Candidate card — all the information that matters, stacked in one place. Application, assessments, notes, history. See the full picture without jumping around.

Design decisions

I pulled every scattered source into one candidate record. Application, tests and interviews, email, history, notes, attachments, each its own tab, so the full context of a candidate sits in one place instead of across different tools.

I kept the team's collaboration on the candidate, not off in another tool. Notes live in the Notes tab, with @mentions to pull the right person in, and the History tab logs who moved a candidate and when. No more ghost decisions, no more "wait, who rejected this person?"

Business & UX impact
  • Tickets about unexplained candidate moves dropped once the History tab logged every action with a name and a time.
  • The single-record view turned into a selling point in demos, instead of scattered context being something to explain away.
(04)Assessment flow

Clarity over options

The problem

The product started with one skills test. Then it grew to four ways to assess someone: a skills test, an interview, an async interview, a homework task. The old setup piled all of that onto one option-heavy screen, and people bailed before they finished building anything.

The insight

Nobody likes a blank canvas. People move faster reacting to a starting point than building from scratch, and they want a little proof they're choosing right before they commit. Templates and a "best for" line on every option give them both: a path to follow and the reassurance to follow it.

↑ Assessment setup — two questions instead of one overwhelming screen. What assessment type, and how do you want to build it? Templates lead, because blank pages are daunting.

Design decisions

I turned the setup into two plain questions on one screen, each doing one job.

First: how do you want to assess candidates in this stage? Four types, each with a short "best for" line, so the choice is obvious instead of a guess. The most common one, the skills test, is flagged popular, so anyone unsure has a default to reach for.

Then: how do you want to build it? Three routes, led by templates, because a starting point beats a blank page. Custom build and copy-from-another-job sit alongside for the people who already know what they want.

Business & UX impact
  • Setup drop-off fell after the redesign, measured across the full funnel.
(05)Analytics & insights

Raw data is noise

The problem

As a screening tool, top-of-funnel numbers were enough. You wanted to know how many people dropped off your test. As a full-cycle ATS, that stopped covering it. Leadership needed the macro picture to report up, and hiring managers needed the micro picture to find where the pipeline was leaking. One screen had to serve both.

The insight

A dashboard that tries to surface everything surfaces nothing. Managers didn't want raw data, they wanted to see exactly where a pipeline was stalling or bleeding candidates. The job of analytics is to point, not to dump.

↑ Analytics — workspace summary, job performance, time-to-fill, time-to-hire. The data that hiring managers actually need to see.

Design decisions

I built two views for two needs, and cut everything that didn't serve them.

The workspace summary answers "how's hiring going?" at a glance: active, paused, and closed openings, average time to fill, average time to hire. The numbers a leader reports up, nothing more.

The per-job drilldown answers "where are we losing people?": active versus rejected candidates per stage, new-candidate volume over time, and how long the role's been open. The per-stage bars make a leaky stage obvious, the one where active shrinks and rejected balloons.

Business & UX impact
  • Managers stopped rebuilding reports in Excel. The full hiring picture lived in the product instead.
(06)Judgment over polish

Four years of this turned Toggl Hire from a single-feature screening tool into a full ATS that competed in a crowded B2B market.

Most ATS products have a design team. This one had me.

Over four years I took Toggl Hire from a single-feature screening tool to a full ATS. The pipeline, the candidate view, the assessments, the analytics, the components underneath, and plenty I'm not showing here.

A product that big, spread across several apps, means perfection across all of it was never on the table. So the real job was judgment: knowing where quality had to hold and where good enough kept things moving. Coming from the business side, I never mistook polish for value. I made those calls fast and kept the product shipping.

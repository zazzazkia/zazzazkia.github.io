(01)Brief

It grew. It stayed simple.

For four years I owned the candidate side of Toggl Hire on my own. It started as one skills test and kept growing: async interviews, homework, a careers page and a full application flow when we pivoted to ATS. The hard part was never adding the features. It was adding all of them without the experience ever getting heavier or more confusing. Every new piece had to feel like it had always been there. Since candidates applied from anywhere, it had to work on every device.

Around a million candidates went through it, with satisfaction holding at 80–90%. A million people voting with their time.

(02)Application

The front door

The problem

When we pivoted to ATS, the hiring flow needed a front door. There was no careers page, no job description, no application form. Nothing to bring a candidate in before the process could even start.

The insight

An unsure candidate is an abandoning candidate. Every unclear step, every guessed field, is a lost applicant for the employer on the other side.

Design decisions

I built the whole entry path as a seamless flow from the careers page to the application form itself. Each step has one job: get the candidate in without friction.

The form itself I kept deliberately simple. A form is not the place to get creative. The real design work went into feedback: clear errors, expected formats shown upfront, no guessing what we need from them.

(03)Skills test

The core

The skills test was the core, the thing everything else grew around. It started as a literal Google Form, and it worked so well that people who weren't even job-hunting would take one just because it showed up on social. A quiz is hard to scroll past.

↑ The skills test out in the wild, shared on social.

When I came on, the experience was already built around candidate control. Skip a question, come back later, review before submitting, get instant feedback. My job wasn't to reinvent that, just to keep it sharp as the product grew. The discipline was knowing what not to touch.

The problem

The timer became a weak spot. It was there, but too quiet, and candidates kept getting caught off guard when time ran out.

Design decisions

I made the timer visible without making it stressful: calm and green, then a red blink when time got tight. Awareness without anxiety.

↑ The same skills test on desktop and mobile.

Business & UX impact
  • Ticket volume from candidates reporting they'd missed the timer running out dropped.
(04)Async interview

Own your time

The problem

First-round interviews are a scheduling dance: finding a slot across time zones, blocking calendars, all to ask five screening questions. Async interviews replaced that, but they traded one obstacle for two others. Recording yourself on camera is nerve-wracking, and a failed upload at the end could wipe out the whole effort.

The insight

The scheduling fix was the easy part: candidates recorded on their own time, employers reviewed on theirs. The real design work was everything that fix didn't touch, the nerves of being on camera, and the risk of losing an entire interview to a stalled upload.

Design decisions

For the nerves, it opened with an intro video from the employer, so a real face came first. Then a camera and mic check before the real test. Questions came one at a time, and like the skills test, the candidate stayed in control: any take could be re-recorded, as often as the clock allowed.

For the uploads, I added feedback at every step: a clear confirmation before submitting, visible progress as each video uploaded, and a “your videos are in” at the end. When an upload failed, a clear error and a “try again” meant candidates never had to re-record the whole interview from scratch.

↑ Intro video, device check, recording and upload states

Business & UX impact
  • Ticket volume from employers reporting videos didn't come through dropped.
  • Tickets from candidates reporting a crashed upload and a lost video dropped too.
(05)Homework

Room to think

The problem

The skills test was built for quick, in-the-moment knowledge checks: timed, and watching for people leaving the tab, because that usually meant cheating. Longer, more analytical work needed the opposite: real time to think, without treating every tab switch like suspicion.

The insight

Not every assessment tests what you know on the spot. Some test how you think something through, and that takes hours, not minutes, and a different kind of trust.

Design decisions

Homework reused the skills-test flow rather than building something new. I dropped the tab-leave tracker, since watching for cheating doesn't fit a task meant to take hours, and added a file uploader for candidates who preferred submitting a document over typing into the editor.

↑ Homework — the same test screen, just open-ended answers.
